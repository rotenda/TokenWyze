What It Means for a South African Stablecoin to Be "Licensed"

The shorthand is everywhere. It is also wrong, and the gap between what it suggests and what it delivers is where a holder's actual protection lives.

July 2026 12 min read Tokenomics Series: SA Regulatory Analysis ZARSC · Stablecoins · FSCA · IFWG

You will see it in headlines, in pitch decks, and in the marketing copy of more than one rand-backed token: South Africa's licensed rand stablecoin. It is easy to reach for, it sounds reassuring, and it is the kind of phrase that ends an argument rather than starting one. It is also a category error, and an instructive one. South Africa does not currently license stablecoins. It licenses certain financial service providers, some of whom happen to issue stablecoins. Those are different things, and the difference is not academic, it determines what recourse you have when something goes wrong.

What the 2022 Declaration Actually Did

In October 2022, by general notice under the Financial Advisory and Intermediary Services Act, the FSCA declared crypto assets to be a financial product. The definition is deliberately wide: a digital representation of value, not issued by a central bank, that can be traded, transferred or stored electronically, using cryptographic techniques and distributed ledger technology. A rand-pegged stablecoin sits inside that definition without difficulty.

What followed was a licensing regime for crypto asset service providers. Applications opened in June 2023, and the FSCA has since worked through a substantial volume of them, several hundred approved, a smaller number declined, and a significant tranche withdrawn after engagement with the regulator. By any comparative measure, South Africa moved faster and licensed more providers than most jurisdictions that started at the same time.

But read what the regime is aimed at. FAIS is conduct legislation. It regulates the rendering of financial services, specifically advice and intermediary services, by persons. A Category I licence permits a provider to advise on and deal in a financial product. A Category II licence permits discretionary management. In each case the licence attaches to the provider and to its conduct in dealing with clients. Nothing in that architecture licenses an instrument.

The honest formulation is: issued by a licensed provider. Not: a licensed stablecoin. Four words apart, and a completely different claim.

What a CASP Licence Does Tell You

This is not nothing, and it would be a mistake to swing from overstatement to dismissal. A licensed provider has satisfied the FSCA that it meets fit-and-proper requirements: honesty and integrity, competence, operational ability, and financial soundness. Key individuals must be appropriately qualified. The FSCA has been explicit that applications fail on exactly these grounds, with inadequate operational frameworks and insufficient demonstrated competence as recurring reasons for refusal.

A licensed provider is also an accountable institution under the Financial Intelligence Centre Act. That brings customer due diligence, transaction monitoring, suspicious transaction reporting, and, since the Travel Rule directive took effect in April 2025, obligations to transmit originator and beneficiary information alongside transfers. A client of a licensed provider has recourse. Conduct failures can be taken to the FSCA and, in appropriate cases, to the relevant ombud.

So the licence tells you something real about the firm: that it has been assessed, that it is supervised, that it carries AML obligations, and that there is somewhere to complain.

What It Does Not Tell You

Here is the part the shorthand obscures. Because the licence attaches to the provider and not to the token, none of the following is currently imposed by South African law as a condition of issuing a rand-pegged stablecoin:

The gaps in the current framework
No reserve requirement
No regulation specifies what must back the token, in what proportion, in what instruments, or where those assets must sit.
No redemption right
Nothing guarantees that a holder can present the token and receive a rand at par, on demand, from the issuer.
No segregation of backing assets
No rule requires reserves to be held separately from the issuer's own funds, or in a structure that survives the issuer's insolvency.
No bankruptcy remoteness
If the issuer fails, the holder's position depends entirely on how the arrangement was privately structured, not on any statutory protection.
No independent attestation
No mandated audit, no required publication schedule, no prescribed form for proving the reserves exist.
No prudential capital requirement
The financial soundness test under FAIS is a solvency test applied to the provider as a services business. It is not a capital requirement calibrated to the size of an outstanding token float.
No supervision of the peg
No regulator is currently tasked with monitoring whether the peg holds, or intervening if it does not.

Some issuers do several of these things voluntarily and do them well. Reserves held at a major commercial bank, independent audits, published attestations, bankruptcy-remote special purpose vehicles, these exist in the local market. But they exist as commercial choices by individual issuers, not as regulatory floors. The distinction matters enormously, because a voluntary practice can be changed by the issuer, and a regulatory floor cannot.

Two further boundaries worth stating plainly

In May 2026, the SARB, the FSCA, the Prudential Authority and the Financial Intelligence Centre issued a joint communication confirming that crypto assets fall outside the National Payment System Act, are not money or funds for that Act's purposes, and are not legal tender. The communication was explicit that authorisation under FAIS does not constitute authorisation to provide payment services.

A CASP licence is not a payments licence, and a stablecoin is not money in the legal sense. So what does a holder actually have? A contractual claim against the issuer, on whatever terms the issuer has written, backed by whatever the issuer has chosen to hold, enforceable through the ordinary courts.

What a Framework Would Have to Address

The comparative material here is instructive, because other jurisdictions have already had to answer these questions explicitly. Under the European Union's Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation, a fiat-referenced token cannot be offered to the public unless the issuer is authorised as a credit institution or an electronic money institution. Authorisation is of the issuer in its capacity as issuer, which is precisely the step South Africa has not taken.

MiCA then layers on obligations that map almost exactly onto the gaps above: reserve composition and custody requirements, a right of redemption at par, segregation of backing assets, own-funds requirements scaled to the float, and disclosure through a mandatory white paper. Whether any of that is right for South Africa is a policy question. The point is narrower: these are the questions a stablecoin framework has to resolve, and they are not resolved by a conduct licence aimed at service providers.

Where Things Currently Stand

The Intergovernmental Fintech Working Group published a diagnostic of the South African stablecoin landscape in early 2025, mapping the arrangements then operating and identifying the regulatory gaps: definition, issuance, stabilisation mechanisms, reserve and custody arrangements, and prudential exposure. Every arrangement it identified was issued by a non-bank.

A discussion paper on rand-pegged stablecoin arrangements followed, with public comment closing in May 2026, and work on foreign-currency-pegged arrangements has been signalled to follow. That process is live. Until it concludes and something is enacted, the position is unchanged: there is no stablecoin-specific authorisation regime in South African law.

Why the Precision Is Worth the Trouble

01
For a holder
The licence answers none of the questions that actually matter. Ask instead: who is the issuer, what backs it, where are those assets held, who has attested to them and how recently, and what happens to your claim if the issuer fails.
02
For an issuer
The current gap is both a competitive opportunity and a coming obligation. Voluntary standards adopted now, genuine segregation, real bankruptcy remoteness, published attestations on a fixed cycle, are a differentiator today and a head start on whatever framework eventually arrives.
03
For the market
Sloppy language is expensive. If retail participants believe "licensed" carries prudential protections it does not carry, the first failure will damage confidence in every issuer, including the careful ones. The market has a direct interest in being precise before an incident forces the issue.

The rand stablecoin sector here is small, a handful of arrangements, with total circulation still modest by any international measure. That is exactly the right size at which to get the vocabulary right.
