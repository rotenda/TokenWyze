Wall Street Is Tokenising Money. South African Banks Cannot Afford to Watch.

America's biggest banks just declared war on stablecoins. The battlefield is the deposit and the weapons are blockchain rails. South Africa has its own version of this fight, and the stakes are just as high.

April 2026 11 min read Banking · Tokenised Deposits · SADC · Stablecoins

Something significant happened this week in American banking. JPMorgan Chase, Citigroup, Bank of America, and Wells Fargo announced they are building a shared tokenised deposit network through The Clearing House. The goal is straightforward: give bank deposits the speed, programmability, and 24/7 availability of a stablecoin, without the regulatory and stability risks that come with one. South African banks should be paying very close attention.

What Tokenised Deposits Actually Are

The term sounds technical. The concept is simple. When you deposit money in a bank, the bank records that deposit in its own private ledger system. That system operates during business hours, does not talk natively to ledger systems at other banks, and moving money between banks or across borders requires a chain of intermediaries, each taking a cut, each adding time, each introducing friction that the end customer ultimately pays for.

A tokenised deposit does not change the fundamental nature of the money. Your deposit is still a deposit, still protected by banking law, still sitting on the bank's balance sheet. What changes is the representation of that deposit: instead of a record in a private ledger, it becomes a digital token on a shared blockchain infrastructure. That token can move instantly, settle in seconds, operate at midnight on a Sunday, carry programmable instructions, and be verified by any participant on the network without requiring a phone call to a correspondent bank.

The key distinction the US banks are drawing is between tokenised deposits and stablecoins. A tokenised deposit stays inside the banking system. The money never leaves. The bank remains the custodian. The regulatory framework remains intact.

This distinction is precisely why regulators, central banks, and treasurers prefer tokenised deposits. They get the efficiency of blockchain settlement without having to trust an issuer they cannot audit.

Why America's Banks Are Moving Now

The trigger is the GENIUS Act, US legislation passed in 2025 that gave stablecoins formal regulatory legitimacy for the first time. Banks lobbied against it. They lost. And in losing, they were forced to confront a reality they had been deferring: stablecoins are not going away, and their deposit base is now genuinely at risk.

Assets such as USDT and USDC now facilitate hundreds of billions of dollars in monthly transaction volume, providing nearly instantaneous international transfers and 24/7 accessibility. Research from the Federal Reserve and the European Central Bank has established that widespread stablecoin adoption could gradually erode bank deposit volumes, limiting the ability of banks to provide credit and potentially weakening monetary policy transmission.

What the US banks are building right now
  • JPMorgan Chase: JPM Coin launched on Coinbase's Base network in late 2025, expanding to Canton Network in 2026, positioning as a direct bank deposit claim with on-chain programmability
  • Citigroup: Citi Token Services, integrating tokenised liquidity with 24/7 USD clearing for cross-border instant payments
  • JPMorgan, Citi, Bank of America, Wells Fargo: Shared tokenised deposit network through The Clearing House
  • Cari Network: Regional US banks targeting a consumer-facing tokenised deposit network launch by end of 2026

This is no longer experimentation. It is commercialisation at scale.

The South African Picture

South Africa's banks face a version of this same dynamic, but with a local texture that makes the stakes both higher and the opportunity more distinctive.

Start with the stablecoin market. ZAR Supercoin (ZARSC), developed by Super Group through its subsidiary Super Money, is licensed by the Financial Sector Conduct Authority and backed by rand reserves held in segregated accounts at Absa. It is live on Luno and expanding to other exchanges. A separate institutional offering, ZAR Universal (ZARU), targets corporate and wholesale users. These are not hypothetical products. They are regulated, live, and growing.

Absa has confirmed that in 2026 it will launch its digital asset custody solution to its business and corporate clients in South Africa, and expects to run proof of concepts with clients on both stablecoins and tokenisation as it looks to scale blockchain-based products. A landmark partnership announced in October 2025 between Ripple and Absa marks the launch of institutional-grade crypto custody infrastructure. Absa and FirstRand are also participating in SWIFT's blockchain pilot, a production system spanning 16 countries with timelines targeting late 2026.

These are not peripheral activities. South Africa's major banks are already in the room where these decisions are being made globally. The question is whether being in the room translates into building the infrastructure.

The Cross-Border Problem South African Banks Are Uniquely Positioned to Solve

Here is where the South African opportunity diverges from the US story in an important way. JPMorgan and Citi are building tokenised deposit infrastructure primarily to defend their domestic deposit base against stablecoin competition. That is a defensive move made by institutions that already have fast, cheap domestic payment rails.

South Africa's banks face the same domestic threat, but they also sit at the centre of a cross-border payments problem so severe that it represents a transformation opportunity of a different magnitude entirely.

The SADC cross-border payments problem in numbers
  • The cost of sending remittances from South Africa to Zimbabwe averaged 12.7% in Q1 2024, far exceeding the G20 target of 3% by 2030
  • Intra-SADC remittances can consume 12% to 25% of the principal amount transferred
  • Settlement times range from one to five business days depending on the corridor
  • South Africa is the financial hub of the SADC region, with billions of rand annually flowing to Zimbabwe, Mozambique, Lesotho, and Malawi
  • The majority of remittance volume currently flows through informal channels precisely because formal banking alternatives are too expensive and too slow

A tokenised rand deposit network, built by South Africa's big four banks on shared infrastructure and interoperable across SADC borders, would compress those fees from double digits to fractions of a percent. It would settle in seconds rather than days. It would operate on weekends when factory workers get paid and need to send money home. It would eliminate the informal cash channels that extract fees from the most financially vulnerable participants in these corridors.

This is not a niche product. It is a structural overhaul of the most important payment corridor on the continent, and one that South Africa's banks are uniquely positioned to own.

What the Regulatory Environment Actually Allows

The objection most often raised inside South African bank boardrooms is regulatory uncertainty. It holds less water than it did two years ago.

South Africa implemented a comprehensive framework classifying crypto assets as financial products in June 2023. Crypto Asset Service Providers must obtain licences and comply with oversight from the FSCA and the Financial Intelligence Centre. The country also approved the FATF Travel Rule, aligning its regulatory approach with global standards.

Critically, tokenised deposits are not crypto assets in the sense that FSCA regulation targets. They are a form of regulated bank money, sitting within the existing prudential framework under the Banks Act. The SARB's Intergovernmental Fintech Working Group is actively developing regulatory approaches to stablecoins and tokenisation. A sandbox framework for domestic stablecoins is already in motion.

Globally, 93% of Financial Stability Board members updated their crypto frameworks in 2025, with 88% prioritising stablecoins. South Africa is not navigating this in isolation. The international direction is set. The domestic framework is moving to match it. That is the signal that action is possible, not the signal to continue waiting.

Three Things South African Banks Should Do

The three-point agenda
1
Build a shared tokenised deposit infrastructure

The US consortium works through co-owned payments infrastructure. South Africa has equivalent foundations in PASA and the SAMOS system. A shared tokenised deposit network built by the major banks, coordinated with the SARB, and anchored in existing payment infrastructure would give South African corporates the same capabilities JPMorgan is offering its clients without the dependence on foreign platform providers.

2
Make cross-border SADC settlement the primary use case

This is where South Africa can differentiate from anything being built in the US. The cross-border payments problem across SADC is not a secondary use case. It is the single most compelling application for tokenised deposit infrastructure in this region. A rand-denominated tokenised deposit that settles instantly across SADC corridors at a fraction of the current average cost would be transformative for millions of people and financially material for the banks that build it.

3
Move from proof of concept to live commercial product in 2026

Absa's commitment to running stablecoin and tokenisation proof of concepts in 2026 is the right instinct. But the US consortium will have a live network operational before the end of 2026. When global institutional clients evaluate their banking partners in Africa, they will ask what South African banks can do on-chain. Proof of concept is not an answer. A live product is.

The Real Question

There is a version of this story where South Africa's banks continue to move cautiously, conducting pilots, attending conferences, and issuing statements about digital transformation while the actual infrastructure remains unbuilt. In that version, the rand-denominated stablecoin market grows without bank participation, the SADC remittance corridors continue to be exploited by non-bank alternatives, and South African banks find themselves three years behind when the institutional demand arrives in force.

BlackRock's leadership has compared tokenisation's potential to the rise of the early internet. That comparison cuts both ways. The institutions that treated the internet as an interesting experiment while competitors built internet-native businesses did not recover their market position. The comparison is a warning as much as a promise.

South Africa's banking sector has the talent, the regulatory relationships, the balance sheets, and the geographic position to build tokenised deposit infrastructure that is genuinely world-class. The US banks' announcement is not a reason to accelerate because America is doing it. It is confirmation that the technology, the regulatory framework, and the commercial case have all arrived at the same moment.

That moment does not last forever.
