The Chain Is Not the Register

Why South African tokenised debt has to keep its authoritative record off-chain, and how to build so that it stops mattering when the law changes.

September 2026 13 min read FMA · Securities Law · Architecture · Tokenised Debt

Ask someone building a tokenised bond where the ownership actually lives and you will usually get an answer about the blockchain. The token is the holding. Transfer the token, transfer the security. It is a clean story and it is the reason tokenisation is interesting at all: if the ledger is the register, then settlement and record-keeping stop being two systems that must be reconciled and become one system that cannot disagree with itself. The story is also, in South Africa, not currently true. And the gap between the story and the legal position is not a technicality to be papered over in a term sheet. It determines the architecture.

The Question Is Narrower Than It Sounds

Strip away the vocabulary and there is one question underneath all of this: when the ledger and the register disagree, which one is wrong? Everything else follows. If the ledger is authoritative, a smart contract can enforce transfer restrictions because a failed transfer is a failed transfer of title. If the register is authoritative, the same smart contract enforces nothing legally. It enforces a policy about token movement, and title moves when the register says it moves. The code can look identical in both worlds. The legal consequences are opposite.

Jurisdictions have started answering this explicitly, and the answers have arrived quickly.

Where the World Has Landed

🇨🇭
Switzerland · DLT Act 2021
Ledger-based security under Article 973d
A genuine new category of uncertificated security whose legal existence is the register entry itself. Rights transfer with the token. The ledger is not evidence of title; it is title. The deepest answer any jurisdiction has given, now five years old.
Verdict: Ledger is authoritative by statute.
🇺🇸
United States · SEC Staff Statement Jan 2026
Distributed ledger can form part of the official register
A share remains an equity security whether it sits in a conventional database or on a blockchain. The SEC approved Nasdaq's rule change in March permitting tokenised securities to trade in the same order book as conventional ones. The DTC holds a no-action letter to run a tokenisation service across pre-approved chains.
Verdict: Ledger can be part of, not a replacement for, the register.
🇰🇷
South Korea · January 2026
Electronic Securities Act amended
The National Assembly amended both the Electronic Securities Act and the Capital Markets Act to permit securities to be issued, recorded, and managed on a distributed ledger. Three different legal traditions arriving at the same conclusion within months of each other.
Verdict: Statutory recognition of DLT as securities register.
🇿🇦
South Africa · Financial Markets Act 2012
Silence
The FMA was drafted for a dematerialised world, not a distributed one. Security tokens are not defined. The CSD architecture remains the authoritative record. Project Khokha 2 called for the Act to be updated in 2022. The FMA review continues. No statutory equivalent of Article 973d exists.
Verdict: Register is authoritative. Ledger is not.

South Africa Has Not Said So

The Financial Markets Act 19 of 2012 is the foundation of South Africa's capital markets, and it was drafted for a dematerialised world, not a distributed one. Uncertificated securities are recorded in registers administered through a licensed central securities depository and its participants. That architecture is not incidental to the Act; it is the Act's answer to who is accountable for the accuracy of the record.

What South Africa does have is a functioning conduct perimeter. The FSCA has received over 500 crypto asset service provider applications since June 2023 and licensed roughly 300, which makes South Africa one of the faster-moving jurisdictions on crypto regulation by any comparative measure. But a CASP licence regulates the conduct of a service provider. It does not make a token a security, and it does not make a ledger entry a transfer of title. Those are different questions living in different statutes, and only one of them has been answered.

This produces the odd position South Africa now occupies: comparatively advanced crypto-asset regulation, no securities tokenisation regime. Meanwhile the building has started regardless. The JSE has been discussing tokenised bonds. A R100 million tokenised debt issuance has been settled here, working inside the CASP perimeter and focusing on private credit rather than equities. People are issuing. The statute has not caught up. So the question is not whether to wait. Nobody is waiting. The question is what you are permitted to build in the meantime.

The Architecture the Law Requires

The only permissible architecture under current South African law
The off-chain register is the authoritative record of title. The chain is a derived mirror. When they disagree, the chain is corrected. The register is never adjusted to match the chain.

This is not a compromise. It is what the law requires. A reference implementation of exactly this architecture, a securities register for tokenised private-market debt written to make the design decisions legible in code rather than prose, is available at the GitHub link below. It is not production software. It exists so that the decisions can be inspected and argued with.

Three Decisions That Carry the Weight

Decision 01
The register is event-sourced, not state-stored
Holdings are not a table you update. They are a fold over an append-only sequence of events, issuance, transfer, restriction, distribution, each one immutable once written. Corrections are new events that reverse old ones, never edits to existing records.
Why: A court or regulator never asks "who holds this today." They ask "who held this on 14 March, and what did you know then." A mutable table cannot answer that. A sequence of events reconstructs any historical position exactly, and shows its own corrections rather than hiding them.
Decision 02
Transfer restrictions are enforced at the register, not at the token
Minimum holdings, lock-ups, maximum holder counts, eligible-transferee tests: these exist because private placements depend on exemptions, and an exemption breached is not a compliance annoyance. It is a public offer you did not intend to make and did not register. Enforcing restrictions in a smart contract is tempting and, under current South African law, wrong.
Why: If the token is not title, a smart contract that blocks a transfer has not prevented a transfer of title. It has prevented a token movement while the underlying rights sit wherever the register put them. The restriction engine has to run where the legal effect happens. That is the register.
Decision 03
Reconciliation is deliberately asymmetric
When the register and the chain disagree, the chain is corrected and the register is never adjusted to match. Not as a preference, as a consequence of what the register legally is. If you ever adjust the register to match the chain, you have quietly conceded that the chain is authoritative, and you have done it in an operations script rather than in a statute.
Why: Reconciliation logic has to encode a judgement about what kind of problem it is looking at, and that judgement is legal before it is technical.

Freeze, Not Burn

The sharpest decision in the reconciliation logic concerns addresses holding units with no corresponding register position. Two responses are available:

Wrong answer
Burn the units
Restores the invariant in one operation. Tidier engineering. But burning halts the harm and destroys the evidence. Units held by an unknown address is not a data inconsistency. It is a compliance incident.
Right answer
Freeze the address
Somebody either moved units outside the sanctioned path or there is a defect in the mirror. Either way the correct response is a human investigation, not a script silently closing the file. Freezing halts the harm and preserves the evidence.

That distinction is what the whole architecture is for. Burning silently resolves a problem that needs to be understood. Freezing holds it open until it is.

Why This Survives the Law Changing

The obvious objection is that this is throwaway work. Amend the FMA, recognise ledger-based securities, and the off-chain register becomes redundant infrastructure built to satisfy a constraint that no longer exists.

Look at what the jurisdictions that did legislate actually built. The SEC's position is that the ledger can form part of the official securityholder register, part of, not instead of. The DTC service places tokenised assets inside existing depository infrastructure rather than replacing it. Even Switzerland's Article 973d requires the ledger to meet conditions about integrity, transparency, and the holders' ability to verify their own entries. Nobody who has answered this question has answered it by deleting the register. They have answered it by specifying what a register must do and then permitting a ledger to do it.

An event-sourced register with the restriction logic and the reconciliation semantics already made explicit is precisely the artefact you migrate from. The path from that to a ledger-based register is a change in where the authoritative sequence is written, not a rebuild of what the sequence means.

Building against the current constraint honestly is not a delay. It is the shortest route to being ready when the constraint lifts.

What This Is Not Arguing

This is not an argument that South African law should recognise ledger-based securities, or that it should not. That is a policy question with distributional consequences for the CSD, for participants, for investors relying on an accountable record-keeper, and it belongs to the FMA review and the IFWG.

The argument is narrower and, I think, harder to dispute: the question of where title lives has a determinate answer in South Africa today, that answer is not the chain, and any tokenised debt structure issued here that behaves as though it were the chain has a defect in it that will not surface until the first dispute.
