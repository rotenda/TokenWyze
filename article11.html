Clarity or Infrastructure: Which One Actually Moves First?

Two stories get told about tokenisation and regulation. The evidence from jurisdictions that have already run the experiment supports neither.

July 2026 11 min read Regulation · DLT · Market Structure · FMA Review

There is a familiar argument in digital asset circles that regulation is a brake, that the technology is arriving regardless, and that the firms building settlement infrastructure are effectively writing the rules the law will eventually ratify. There is an equally familiar argument from the other direction: that institutional capital is waiting on the sidelines for legal certainty, and that the moment a framework arrives, the volume follows. Both are testable. Several jurisdictions have now supplied the test, and the results are more interesting than either camp tends to acknowledge.

The Cleanest Experiment: The EU's DLT Pilot Regime

If you wanted to design an experiment to isolate the effect of regulatory clarity on tokenised market infrastructure, you would build something very close to what the European Union built. The DLT Pilot Regime became effective in March 2023. It does something unusual: rather than merely permitting DLT, it grants targeted exemptions from the rules that otherwise make tokenised securities infrastructure impossible to operate, specifically relief from aspects of MiFID and from the Central Securities Depositories Regulation. It creates three categories of DLT market infrastructure and lets national competent authorities authorise them case by case.

In other words: regulators identified the legal obstacles to tokenised securities settlement, and then removed them, on purpose, in a supervised environment.

Case study: EU DLT Pilot Regime
Clarity supplied. The market largely did not come.
In its first year, ESMA reported that no infrastructure had yet been authorised and only four applications had been submitted. By the time it published its comprehensive review in June 2025, three entities had been approved. ESMA's recommendation was not to abandon the regime but to make it permanent, more flexible in its thresholds, and broader in the assets it covers, essentially to make it more attractive, because attractiveness had been the problem.
Finding: Legal certainty was supplied. Uptake remained very modest.

The Deepest Clarity: Switzerland

Switzerland went further than anyone, and earlier. The DLT Act came into force in two phases across 2021, amending ten federal laws rather than creating a separate regime alongside them. The centrepiece is the ledger-based security under Article 973d of the Code of Obligations, a genuinely new category of uncertificated security whose legal existence is the register entry itself. Rights transfer by transfer of the token. Nearly any right capable of being securitised can be structured this way, including receivables and, subject to company law, shares.

This is the answer to the question raised in this publication before: whether an on-chain register can be the authoritative legal record. Switzerland said yes, in statute, five years ago.

Case study: Switzerland DLT Act 2021
The most comprehensive statutory answer to the on-chain register question. Results: real, but incremental.
Swiss SMEs have tokenised shares. The Capital Markets and Technology Association published a tokenisation standard in December 2021 and has maintained it since. Activity is genuine and growing. It is not a transformation of Swiss capital markets, and five years is long enough that we would expect to see one if legal certainty were the binding constraint.
Finding: Comprehensive clarity produced real activity but not the transformation the advocacy predicted.

The Inverse Case: South Africa

South Africa ran the experiment from the other end, largely by accident. Crypto assets were declared a financial product in 2022 and service provider licensing opened in 2023, with several hundred licences approved since. But there is still no stablecoin framework, the consultation on rand-pegged arrangements closed for comment in May 2026, and the Financial Markets Act has not been amended to address tokenised securities, DLT-based market infrastructure, or the status of on-chain registers. That review is underway.

Despite this, roughly ten rand-referenced stablecoin arrangements operate locally, and a tokenised bond was issued and settled here in 2024. On the surface, this looks like strong evidence for the permissionless thesis: infrastructure got built while the framework was still being drafted. But look at how it was actually done.

What "Without Permission" Actually Means in Practice

The tokenised bond was not issued in a legal vacuum. It was issued under a prospectus registered with the CIPC, in accordance with the Companies Act. The instrument was an ordinary debenture. The token was a representation of it. That is not building without permission. That is building inside a body of law that was already clear, and using DLT for the parts that law does not govern.

The honest version of "we are not waiting for permission" is usually "we found a corner of the existing framework where permission was already granted." That is a much less dramatic claim, and a much more useful one, because it tells you where to look.

The same pattern holds almost everywhere. Swiss tokenisation runs on Article 973d, a statute. Tokenised money market funds are issued under existing fund regimes. Tokenised treasuries are treasuries, the legal wrapper is conventional and the ledger is the innovation. What practitioners actually do, over and over, is locate the part of existing law that already provides certainty and build there.

The corollary matters too. When people say infrastructure providers are writing the rules, what is generally happening is that infrastructure providers are demonstrating what is technically possible, and regulators are subsequently deciding which of those possibilities to accommodate. Switzerland codified after observing. MiCA arrived after the market had formed. The sequence is real, but it is not the same as private networks setting the rules.

So What Actually Binds?

If legal certainty were the constraint, the DLT Pilot Regime would be crowded and Switzerland would have transformed. Neither happened. Three other constraints appear to bind harder.

The three constraints that bind harder than legal clarity
The economics have to work on their own Tokenisation reduces certain costs: reconciliation, transfer administration, settlement latency. It does not reduce credit risk, and it does not conjure yield. If an instrument's spread does not survive contact with the underlying assets, no amount of legal clarity rescues it. Much of the tokenisation that has succeeded commercially has been in instruments where the operational savings are large relative to the margin: money market funds, treasuries, short-dated credit.
Fractionalisation is not liquidity Dividing an illiquid asset into ten thousand units produces ten thousand illiquid holdings and a set of investors who were promised an exit. Secondary markets require someone on the other side of the second trade. This is a market structure problem and legislation does not solve it.
Parallel infrastructure is expensive An institution adopting tokenised settlement does not switch, it runs two systems, reconciles between them, and absorbs the cost of both until volume justifies the migration. The EU experience suggests this is precisely the barrier: firms weighed the cost of standing up a DLT market infrastructure, even with exemptions, against the volume plausibly available, and mostly declined. ESMA's recommended fix was to widen eligible assets and loosen thresholds, that is, to improve the economics, not the clarity.

What This Suggests for the South African Review

The Financial Markets Act review is genuinely important, and the questions it faces are the right ones: whether an on-chain register can be authoritative, whether a tokenised security must sit with a central securities depository, what settlement finality requires when the ledger is distributed, and whether anything resembling a pilot or sandbox accommodation is appropriate.

What the comparative evidence suggests is a calibration of expectations rather than a policy position. Legal certainty appears to be necessary, and the local market's reliance on the Companies Act route rather than the Financial Markets Act route is itself evidence that participants go where the law is settled. But necessary is not sufficient. Jurisdictions that resolved these questions comprehensively did not see the volumes that the advocacy predicted, and they did not see them for reasons that had little to do with law.

The more useful question for anyone building here is not when the framework arrives. It is which instruments have operational costs large enough, relative to their margin, that moving them on-chain pays for itself, and whether there is a plausible buyer for the second trade.

Those are questions about economics and market structure. The law determines whether you are allowed to answer them. It does not answer them for you.
